# Prose patterns

Apply these repairs where they improve the passage. Preserve the requested voice, factual claims, and precise domain meanings.

## Structure and rhetoric

### 1. Stock contrasts and negative framing

Watch `not only X but also Y`, `not just`, `not merely`, `more than just`, `X, not Y`, `This isn't X. It's Y.`, and rhetorical `rather than` tails. State the actual point directly. Keep both supported claims: "Supports not only CSV but also JSON" becomes "Supports CSV and JSON."

Cut unasked explanations of what the subject is not. "This isn't a new paradigm or an overhaul" distracts from a concrete change such as "The picker remembers its last folder." Use only supplied facts. Retain meaningful limits and distinctions, such as "The export includes saved changes, not unsaved edits", and exact phrasing explicitly chosen by the user.

### 2. Denial chains

"No ceremony. No friction. Just flow." and "Not a workaround. Not a compromise. A new paradigm." manufacture emphasis. State the supported behavior. Preserve actual prohibitions and purposeful dialogue.

### 3. Self-answered questions

"The result?", "The catch?", "The secret?", and "Why does this matter?" stage an answer the writer already has. Give that answer directly. Keep genuine FAQ questions that help readers find information.

### 4. Announcements before the point

"Here's the thing", "Let's be honest", "Real talk", "Let's unpack", "Let's dive in", and "Buckle up" usually delay the claim. Start with it. Preserve a supplied anecdote, admission, or invitation that serves the text.

### 5. Emphasis without information

"That matters.", "Read that again.", "Let that sink in.", and "That's the real win." repeat emphasis. Remove them or give the distinct consequence they promise.

### 6. Unsupported reframing

"The real issue", "the deeper truth", "fundamentally", "at its core", and "what really matters" promise insight. Name and support the actual issue. Keep corrections that explain why an earlier interpretation failed.

### 7. Forced triads and symmetry

"Fast, focused, flexible", synonymous mini-examples, and identical setup/contrast/closer paragraphs repeat a template. Develop the useful point and cut padding. Preserve real three-item lists, ordered procedures, and deliberate rhetoric.

Use author samples for register and reasoning. Write original, topic-specific openings and examples when creating new material.

### 8. Fragment chains

"Wrong path. Wrong shape. Wrong assumption." can replace explanation with punchlines. Say what failed and why. Keep useful imperatives, labels, dialogue, and occasional purposeful fragments.

### 9. Low information density

Repeated thesis paragraphs, generic benefits, and inflated phrases add words without advancing the point. Test each sentence's contribution: fact, reason, condition, example, instruction, or necessary connection. Merge duplicate claims and keep qualifications that affect meaning.

"In order to export, the tool has the ability to write CSV files" becomes "The tool can export CSV files." Cut abstract descriptions such as "a thoughtfully designed solution for productive workflows" when the source already explains the behavior. Give successive paragraphs distinct jobs; reference sections can remain parallel.

Generic additions such as "keeps the workflow in one focused app" need a specific consequence useful to this reader. A feature heading should name its capability, such as "Compare saved versions", when the source supports it.

For product copy, foreground user-visible behavior. Keep architecture details that affect the reader's decision.

### 10. Repeated roadmaps and recaps

"In this article we'll explore", a heading restated in its first sentence, recaps after every example, and closing summaries of those recaps delay substance. Keep navigation and summaries that help readers use a complex document. End with the last useful fact or action.

## Sentences and claims

### 11. Em-dash overuse

Use zero em dashes in editable prose by default. Choose punctuation by meaning or restructure the sentence. Preserve literal quotations, code, identifiers, proper names, and a requested dash-using voice. Keep numerical en-dash ranges such as `8–12 hours`.

### 12. Empty participle tails

"...highlighting its significance", "...underscoring its importance", "...fostering innovation", and "...ensuring a seamless experience" add interpretation without evidence. Remove them or explain the supported mechanism. "Closing the handle releases the lock" describes actual behavior.

### 13. Noun stacks and invented labels

"Developer experience transformation enablement" and "the workflow confidence layer" hide the action. Name what happens and who benefits. Preserve established domain terms, product names, and concepts the author defines.

Define specialist terms at first use and briefly explain niche mechanisms. Retain technical depth. A term such as `RETURN` may mean leaving a procedure; distinguish that from returning a value when the difference matters.

In instructions, name the application, control, and action using actual labels readers can find.

### 14. Inflated verbs

"Serves as", "stands as", "represents a", "boasts", and "showcases" often inflate `is` or `has`. Use the simplest accurate verb. Keep technical senses such as serving requests or representing a value.

### 15. Vague relationships and authority

"Associated with", "linked to", "experts agree", and "industry leaders recognize" can conceal the relationship or source. State the evidence already available. Flag missing material evidence; retain the source's uncertainty and causal limits.

Match an explanation to what its example demonstrates. Flag examples that need verification; preserve protected code and quoted identifiers during prose editing.

Explain why behavior occurs. An authority's matching example can support the explanation; it supplies no causal mechanism on its own.

### 16. Qualifier piles and automatic counterpoints

"Could potentially perhaps", "it may arguably", habitual "while X, Y", and "to be fair" blur judgment. Keep the qualifier expressing actual uncertainty. Preserve exceptions, scientific uncertainty, disagreement, and tradeoffs at their original strength.

### 17. False ranges and scope

"From productivity to innovation" supplies no defined scale. List actual capabilities. Check `always`, `never`, `every`, `first`, `only`, and `guarantees` against the source. Preserve established all/none contracts and accurate superlatives.

### 18. Synonym cycling

Alternating "the model", "the engine", "the platform", "the solution", and "the system" can imply different objects. Use consistent terminology and precise domain meanings. After revisions, check the opening, examples, and conclusions against the updated explanation.

## Vocabulary and presentation

### 19. Familiar promotional words

Watch abstract uses of `delve`, `leverage`, `foster`, `tapestry`, `landscape`, `ecosystem`, `unlock`, `elevate`, `empower`, `seamless`, `game-changing`, `cutting-edge`, `revolutionary`, `transformative`, `pivotal`, `testament`, and `journey`. State the supported action. Preserve technical meanings such as ecological ecosystems and financial leverage.

### 20. Understated filler

`Quietly`, `genuinely`, `deeply`, `thoughtfully`, `intentional`, `nuanced`, `meaningful`, `robust`, `compelling`, `arguably`, `notably`, and `at scale` often decorate claims. "Quietly redefines the workflow" needs concrete substance. Keep words describing observable behavior or precise concepts, such as robust regression.

### 21. Stock frames

"In today's fast-paced world", "when it comes to", "it's worth noting", "whether you're a beginner or a seasoned professional", and "at the end of the day" delay the subject. Remove them. Retain audience distinctions that change prerequisites or behavior.

### 22. Added excitement and experiences

"We're thrilled", "passionate about", generic vulnerability, and invented debugging stories impose a voice. Match the author's supplied enthusiasm, humor, experiences, and judgments. Use the requested register.

### 23. Excessive decoration

Emoji on every heading, bold labels on every bullet, automatic title case, and excessive microsections fragment the argument. Use formatting that helps navigation, comparison, or steps. Follow document templates and accessibility needs.

### 24. Chat residue

"Great question", "Certainly", "I hope this helps", offers to continue, reviewer exchanges, and narrated validation rounds belong to the conversation. In explanatory prose, state verified behavior directly. Keep useful methodology, measurements, limitations, source credits, and normal salutations.

### 25. Re-explaining context in replies

A reply restates the reported issue, narrates every diagnostic step, and postpones the action. Lead with the decision; retain new evidence and useful links. Give a requested analysis its necessary depth.

### 26. Overused analogies

An opening analogy can make a difficult concept approachable. Reusing its characters or imagery throughout the article can obscure precise behavior. Keep the useful introduction, then develop the technical explanation with literal terms. Preserve the author's humor.

### 27. Off-topic product framing

Describe the product's supported behavior and relevant benefit. Cut unsolicited competitor criticism, praise of another provider, and apologetic justifications. For a requested comparison, use the same feature and supported scope on both sides. A single example cannot establish "everything" or "nothing" across a product.

## Other languages

Apply structural rules by meaning. In German, watch empty `nicht nur ... sondern auch`, `es geht nicht um ... sondern um`, `in der heutigen schnelllebigen Welt`, `es ist wichtig zu betonen`, `tauchen wir ein`, `nahtlos`, and promotional `wegweisend`/`revolutionär`. Judge ordinary terms such as `dabei`, `somit`, `robust`, and `ermöglicht` in context. Follow the language's punctuation and register.
